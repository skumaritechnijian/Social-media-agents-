IT disaster recovery and backup are not the same thing, and conflating them is the most common planning gap I see among Newport Beach businesses. Backup preserves your data. Disaster recovery restores your entire IT operation including systems, applications, network infrastructure, and cloud services to a functional state within a defined timeframe.

A workable Newport Beach DR plan starts with two numbers. Your Recovery Time Objective (RTO) is the maximum downtime you can absorb before the business becomes untenable. Your Recovery Point Objective (RPO) is the maximum data loss measured in time. For most Newport Beach professional services firms, realistic targets are an RTO of four to eight hours for critical systems and an RPO of one to four hours.

Once you have those numbers, classify your systems into tiers. Tier 1 is client-facing and must recover within RTO: CRM, email, financial platforms, EHR. Tier 2 is important but can wait twenty-four hours: collaboration tools, analytics platforms. Tier 3 is non-critical and can wait seventy-two hours: legacy archives, development environments. Each tier needs different backup frequency and different recovery infrastructure.

Recovery infrastructure follows three models. Cold standby backs up data offsite but requires provisioning from scratch (cheap, slow). Warm standby keeps a partially configured environment ready (moderate cost, hours to recover). Hot standby runs a fully synchronized duplicate environment (highest cost, near-instant failover). Cloud platforms have made hot standby economically reachable for Newport Beach SMBs that previously could not afford a secondary data center.

The plan must be documented and tested. FINRA, HIPAA, and SOC 2 auditors all require evidence of testing. Annual full failover tests are the standard, with semi-annual tabletop exercises for each scenario type.

For Newport Beach specifically, plan for wildfire-driven evacuations, coastal power vulnerabilities, ransomware attacks, and SaaS outages. Each requires a different playbook.
