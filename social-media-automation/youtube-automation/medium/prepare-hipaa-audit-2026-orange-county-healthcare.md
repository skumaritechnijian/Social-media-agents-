# How to Prepare for a HIPAA Audit in 2026: The Complete OC Healthcare Checklist

For Orange County healthcare practices, HIPAA audit readiness is not just about preparing for a formal audit letter. It is about being able to show, at any time, that privacy, security, breach notification, and vendor oversight are active parts of the business.

The HHS Office for Civil Rights (OCR) says its HIPAA Audit Program reviews covered entities and business associates for compliance with the HIPAA Privacy, Security, and Breach Notification Rules. OCR's 2024-2025 audit cycle focused on selected Security Rule provisions tied to hacking and ransomware risk, a useful signal for what healthcare organizations should prioritize heading into 2026.

For local practices, the risk is broader than a scheduled audit. A breach report, patient privacy complaint, vendor incident, ransomware event, or missing Business Associate Agreement can all create regulatory exposure. The difference between a practice that handles OCR scrutiny calmly and one that scrambles is usually the quality of its documentation before anything goes wrong.

This checklist covers the practical areas OCR evaluates and the action items Orange County healthcare practices should keep current.

## Understanding What OCR Reviews

OCR uses HIPAA audits and compliance reviews to examine whether covered entities and business associates have the right policies, safeguards, documentation, and processes in place. The audit protocol covers the Privacy Rule, Security Rule, and Breach Notification Rule.

For most small and mid-sized practices, the highest-risk areas are familiar:

- Failure to conduct an accurate and thorough Security Risk Analysis
- Weak access controls and workforce authentication practices
- Missing or outdated Business Associate Agreements
- Incomplete workforce training records
- Unclear incident response and breach notification procedures
- Backup, disaster recovery, and contingency planning gaps

## The HIPAA Audit Preparation Checklist for OC Practices

## Section 1: Security Risk Analysis

The Security Risk Analysis is the foundation of HIPAA Security Rule compliance. OCR's audit protocol expects organizations to assess potential risks and vulnerabilities to the confidentiality, integrity, and availability of electronic protected health information.

An effective analysis should identify systems that create, receive, maintain, or transmit ePHI, evaluate threats and vulnerabilities, document existing controls, assess likelihood and impact, and track remediation priorities. It should also be updated when major operational or technical changes occur, such as a new EHR, cloud migration, acquisition, or remote work expansion.

- Conduct a full Security Risk Analysis covering all ePHI systems.
- Document identified risks with likelihood, impact, owner, and remediation status.
- Maintain a risk register that tracks progress over time.
- Keep evidence of the methodology used and who performed the analysis.
- Review the analysis after major technology, staffing, vendor, or workflow changes.

## Section 2: Administrative Safeguards

Administrative safeguards are the policies, procedures, responsibilities, and workforce practices that govern how ePHI is protected. OCR can evaluate these safeguards through documentation requests, management interviews, training records, and evidence of ongoing compliance activity.

- Maintain a current HIPAA Security Officer designation in writing.
- Document workforce authorization procedures that define who can access each ePHI system.
- Keep signed HIPAA training completion records for all workforce members.
- Update workforce training at least annually and after material policy or risk changes.
- Maintain a sanction policy for workforce members who violate HIPAA policies.
- Review and version-control HIPAA policies and procedures.
- Document contingency planning, including data backup, disaster recovery, and emergency mode operations.

## Section 3: Physical Safeguards

Physical safeguards govern access to the facilities, workstations, devices, and media where ePHI is stored or accessed. Even cloud-first practices still need physical safeguards for endpoints, front-desk workstations, mobile devices, network equipment, and paper workflows.

- Document facility access controls, including keys, alarm codes, badges, and access to server or network areas.
- Maintain workstation use policies defining where and how workforce members may access ePHI.
- Document workstation security controls, including screen locks, privacy screens, and clean desk expectations.
- Maintain media controls for USB drives, backup drives, retired devices, printed records, and disposal workflows.
- Keep evidence for device encryption, secure disposal, and asset tracking.

## Section 4: Technical Safeguards

Technical safeguards are the technology controls that protect ePHI. This is where many small practices have the most urgent gaps, especially after years of remote access, cloud adoption, vendor integrations, and ransomware targeting.

- Require unique user IDs for all workforce members.
- Eliminate shared accounts and shared passwords.
- Implement multi-factor authentication for EHR, email, remote access, billing, cloud storage, and administrative accounts.
- Enable audit logging on systems that access or store ePHI.
- Review logs and alerts through a documented process.
- Encrypt ePHI in transit using modern TLS standards.
- Encrypt ePHI at rest on endpoints, servers, backups, and removable media.
- Configure automatic logoff or screen lock settings for ePHI workstations.
- Maintain endpoint protection, patch management, and vulnerability remediation records.

## Section 5: Business Associate Agreements

Business Associate Agreement gaps are common because vendor lists change faster than compliance binders. Billing vendors, IT providers, cloud storage platforms, EHR vendors, marketing platforms, shredding providers, answering services, and consultants may all need to be evaluated.

- Compile a complete inventory of vendors and service providers that create, receive, maintain, or transmit PHI or ePHI.
- Confirm a signed, current BAA is on file for every required vendor.
- Review BAAs for breach notification duties, permitted uses and disclosures, safeguard obligations, and subcontractor requirements.
- Confirm cloud providers have an executed BAA before storing ePHI in their services.
- Flag any ePHI flow without a BAA and remediate immediately.

## Section 6: Breach Notification Documentation

OCR evaluates whether breach notification duties are understood, documented, and followed. Covered entities must notify HHS after discovering a breach of unsecured protected health information, and timing depends on the number of individuals affected.

For incidents affecting 500 or more individuals, notification to HHS is due without unreasonable delay and no later than 60 calendar days after discovery. Smaller breaches are still reportable, typically no later than 60 days after the end of the calendar year in which they were discovered.

- Maintain a breach and security incident log.
- Document the assessment for every privacy or security incident, even when it is not ultimately reportable.
- Keep notification procedures for patients, HHS, business associates, and media notification when required.
- Test the incident response workflow so staff know who to call and what to preserve.
- Store evidence of investigations, mitigation, and corrective actions.

## Section 7: Incident Response and Workforce Readiness

A plan that has never been tested will not help much during a real incident. OCR looks for signs that organizations can identify, respond to, mitigate, document, and learn from security events.

- Maintain a written incident response plan.
- Test the plan at least annually with a tabletop exercise or live drill.
- Define roles for leadership, IT, compliance, legal, communications, and vendor contacts.
- Document investigations and outcomes for security incidents.
- Provide role-specific training for workforce members with elevated access.
- Review lessons learned and update policies after incidents or exercises.

## The Biggest HIPAA Audit Mistake OC Practices Make

The most common mistake is treating HIPAA compliance as a one-time project instead of an active program. A practice may complete an SRA once, draft policies once, train employees once, and then assume the work is finished.

That approach creates risk because OCR is not only looking for the existence of controls. It is looking for evidence that the practice understands its obligations, monitors risk, updates documentation, trains the workforce, manages vendors, and responds appropriately when something changes.

A documented program with tracked remediation is stronger than a collection of informal good intentions. In an audit or investigation, evidence matters.
